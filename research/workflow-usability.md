# CertCity workflow usability

The first usability observation came from a player trying the City Dispatch repair. The diagram’s **Recovery rules** node and the **Recovery rules** editor shared a name, but the node did not lead anywhere, and the editor did not identify which setting affected T-17. On a narrow screen, the two views were also separated by scrolling. That made players infer both where to act and whether an edit affected the ticket.

| Design decision | Evidence and application |
|---|---|
| Make the diagram actionable. | Direct-manipulation research describes the value of acting on a visible object and immediately seeing how it changes. The diagram node is now a button with an explicit edit label. It focuses **Response contract** while the policy is shared, or **Temporary timeout** when the player routes by outcome. [Hutchins, Hollan and Norman, 1985](https://hci.ucsd.edu/hollan/direct-manip.pdf) |
| Put the connection beside the setting. | Chandler and Sweller’s split-attention experiments found benefits when learners could read mutually dependent information together. The editor labels the active setting **Used by T-17** and shows the live ticket outcome in the same panel. The diagram displays the exact option label. Both previews use the same rule and result. The shift run still tests the policy across 1,000 requests. [Chandler and Sweller, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1992.tb01017.x) |
| Show the objective and first action at entry. | A meta-analysis found benefits from feedback and scaffolding in discovery tasks. The assignment now states what a successful shift looks like and guides the player from the first replay to editing and saving. The exam-style check keeps its own scenario and reveals feedback after submission. [Alfieri et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017) |
| Make links and feedback work without color. | The linked setting has a text label, the active diagram stage exposes its state, and the live outcome is announced as a status. Activating the diagram button focuses the relevant control, with scroll spacing for the sticky dialog header. Editing a dropdown keeps focus on the same control. [W3C use of color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html) · [W3C status messages](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html) · [W3C focus visibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html) · [W3C predictable input](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/on-input.html) |

## First-time playtest

Use the small rounds already scoped in the [MVP evidence plan](G-mvp-plan.md). Include new certification candidates with different levels of MCP knowledge, and cover keyboard and touch alongside mouse. Start on the city screen. Ask participants to find what to do next and make the depot stop reporting a missing shipment when its lookup service is unavailable. Let them work without naming the control or pointing at the node. Then ask them to explain which setting affected T-17, how they verified it, and what the city’s time and spend counters mean. Resume the prep pathway and ask them to find the next step. [GOV.UK moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing)

Record the first action, time to the first relevant edit, completion without a hint, and the participant’s explanation of the link between the editor and the diagram. Watch for confusion between the live preview and saved impact, and between module steps and full exam coverage. Browser interaction tests verify the mechanics and layout.
